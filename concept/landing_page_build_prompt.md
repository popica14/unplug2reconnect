# Build Prompt: Unplug to Reconnect Landing Page

## Your role

Act as a senior creative developer, digital art director, UX designer, and conversion-minded copy editor. Design and build a distinctive, production-quality landing page for **Unplug to Reconnect**.

This is not a generic outdoor retreat, a luxury glamping holiday, an anti-technology campaign, or a childish summer camp. It is a small, thoughtful, one-week pilot experience for young adults aged 18–25 in a real rural community in the Romanian Carpathians.

The finished page should make the right person think:

> This feels honest, different, slightly challenging, and made for people like me. I want to know when the first week happens.

## Core idea

**One week offline. One week of real life.**

For seven days, 10–12 young adults and two hosts step away from digital noise and share ordinary, physical life: hiking, cooking, learning useful skills, debating meaningful subjects, helping to run camp, sitting with unstructured time, and spending one night in the mountains.

The project does not reject technology or promise instant transformation. It creates the conditions for three grounded outcomes:

1. **Digital reset** — technology moves away from the center of daily life.
2. **Confidence** — participants learn by doing, carry responsibility, and discover what they can handle.
3. **Real human connection** — people live, work, disagree, laugh, and talk face-to-face.

The common language is English. Participants may come from Romania, Germany, and elsewhere. Phones are stored safely, remain available when needed, and can be used during designated contact times. The experience is challenging but not survival training; safety, appropriate equipment, and experienced leadership are fundamental.

## Primary goal

Create emotional recognition and trust, then convert interested visitors into **pilot updates / expressions of interest**.

Use one primary CTA consistently:

**Join the pilot list**

Use a lower-commitment secondary CTA where useful:

**See the week**

Do not invent dates, prices, testimonials, partner logos, certifications, participant statistics, or a confirmed location. Where operational details are not yet known, say so plainly or use clearly marked content placeholders in the implementation.

## Audience

The primary visitor is 18–25, internationally minded, digitally saturated, curious, and wary of both corporate wellness language and forced inspiration. They may feel distracted, socially isolated, uncertain about adulthood, or simply hungry for something tangible and real.

The page should also reassure a secondary audience—parents or trusted adults—without changing the youthful tone. Clear safety and practical information should do that work quietly.

## Creative direction

Build around this visual equation:

**Field manual × independent magazine × mountain expedition × youth culture**

The page should feel tactile, editorial, raw, warm, intelligent, and contemporary. Think folded maps, field notes, equipment labels, old digital-camera timestamps, weathered paper, documentary contact sheets, and a route traced by hand. The layout may be precise, but it should never feel sterile.

### Signal / No Signal

The central visual concept combines fragments of digital life with the physical outdoors. Use restrained interface-like details—signal bars, battery indicators, coordinates, timestamps, system labels, switches, status readouts—alongside mountains, topographic contours, paper, canvas, wood, mud, fire, maps, handwriting, and practical objects.

The message is not “technology is bad.” It is:

> We came from digital life. Now we are here.

Avoid crossed-out phones, crossed-out Wi-Fi symbols, generic leaf logos, glossy travel imagery, glassmorphism, neon gradients, corporate SaaS layouts, wellness clichés, military styling, and survivalist machismo.

## Visual system

Use the following palette as design tokens:

| Token | Value | Purpose |
|---|---:|---|
| Charcoal | `#20211E` | Main text, night fields, equipment labels |
| Bone | `#EEE9DE` | Primary paper/canvas background |
| Forest | `#32483B` | Nature fields and structural contrast |
| Clay | `#A55236` | Earthy accents and stamps |
| Fire | `#E87535` | Primary CTA and moments of energy |
| Sky | `#9EB4B7` | Mist, distance, cool secondary areas |

Keep colors muted, weathered, and physical. Ensure accessible contrast, especially for body text and controls.

Typography should have three distinct voices:

- A **bold condensed sans** for the wordmark and major headlines. Prefer Barlow Condensed, Archivo Narrow, Roboto Condensed, or League Gothic.
- A **clean humanist sans** for readable body copy. Prefer Inter, IBM Plex Sans, or Source Sans 3.
- A **monospace accent** for coordinates, timestamps, labels, and digital traces. Prefer IBM Plex Mono or Space Mono.

Treat typography as a major visual material. Use oversized, tightly composed statements, compact practical labels, and a readable editorial rhythm. Do not make every heading the same size or every section the same shape.

Use subtle recurring graphic devices:

- topographic contour lines;
- map routes and directional arrows;
- coordinates and timestamps;
- checkboxes, stamps, equipment tags, and field-note annotations;
- the switch motif `DIGITAL ○────● ANALOGUE`;
- a minimal signal-bars-meet-mountain symbol if a small brand mark is needed.

These details should support hierarchy and storytelling, not become decorative clutter.

## Photography direction

Photography must feel documentary rather than promotional. Prioritize candid, imperfect moments: wet boots, dirty hands, food preparation, backpacks, route planning, laughter, smoke, cards, tents, morning fog, practical work, and quiet pauses.

Use grain, occasional direct flash, motion blur, crooked framing, overexposure, timestamps, and contact-sheet crops with restraint. The people should look like participants, not models. Show a range of personalities and backgrounds without turning diversity into a staged visual claim.

If final photography is unavailable, use clearly labeled image placeholders with specific shot directions. Do not silently substitute generic luxury-cabin or influencer-hiking stock photography. Build image components so participant-shot digital-camera images can replace placeholders later without redesigning the page.

## Page narrative and sections

The experience should unfold as a journey from digital noise to physical presence. Do not simply stack interchangeable feature cards.

### 1. Navigation

Create a compact, practical header with the wordmark, anchor links, and the primary CTA. Suggested anchors:

- The week
- What you do
- The expedition
- Practical details
- FAQ

The mobile navigation must remain simple and usable. Avoid a large, app-like navigation system.

### 2. Hero: leave the feed

Lead with:

**ONE WEEK OFFLINE.  
ONE WEEK OF REAL LIFE.**

Support it with concise copy explaining the proposition:

> Seven days in the Romanian Carpathians to use your hands, walk into the mountains, meet people face-to-face, and remember what happens when no algorithm decides what comes next.

Include:

- `10–12 PEOPLE / 7 DAYS / CARPATHIANS, RO`;
- primary CTA: **Join the pilot list**;
- secondary CTA: **See the week**;
- a restrained `SIGNAL / NO SIGNAL` visual cue;
- a strong documentary image or art-directed placeholder.

The first viewport should be memorable and legible, not overloaded. Avoid full-screen autoplay video.

### 3. The proposition: less digital, more analogue

Explain the philosophy without preaching. Technology is not the enemy; this is an intentional experiment in moving it out of the center for a few days.

Use the three outcomes as an editorial triptych:

- **Digital reset**
- **Confidence**
- **Real human connection**

Give each one concrete evidence from the week rather than vague benefits.

### 4. Seven-day field log

Create a tactile, scrollable or vertically sequenced “field log” that makes the week imaginable. It need not claim a fixed curriculum. Show the rhythm instead:

- slow breakfast;
- morning practical or discussion workshop;
- cooking and eating together;
- unstructured afternoon time;
- shared responsibility for camp;
- evenings around the fire;
- one overnight mountain expedition.

Use timestamps, day markers, handwritten notes, or map progress. Include moments of purposeful emptiness—reading, sleeping, cards, walking, or doing nothing. Make it clear that boredom is allowed.

### 5. Learn by doing / think by talking

Use a strong split composition rather than a generic icon grid.

On one side, practical workshop examples:

- fire building;
- mountain navigation;
- hike planning;
- camp setup;
- cooking and nutrition;
- woodworking and traditional skills.

On the other, discussion themes:

- relationships and identity;
- climate and consumption;
- society, politics, and responsibility;
- success and the choices of early adulthood;
- social media and its influence.

State clearly that these are examples, not a fixed curriculum. The hosts create a respectful environment; they do not prescribe the answers.

### 6. The mountain night

Give the overnight expedition its own cinematic, high-contrast section. Tell the physical sequence:

> Pack at camp → leave on foot → carry what you need → establish camp → watch the stars → wake for sunrise → walk home.

Clarify that proper backpacks, reliable tents, and sleeping bags suitable for temperatures around 0°C are provided. Balance challenge with reassurance: intentional discomfort, competent planning, and experienced leadership—not survival training.

### 7. Phones down. Cameras up.

Turn the phone policy into a memorable interaction or visual story. Explain that smartphones are stored safely and are available when needed, with designated opportunities to contact family and friends and emergency access at all times.

Introduce the digital-camera idea:

> Capture the experience without experiencing it through a smartphone.

Consider a contact sheet or camera-frame gallery that can eventually feature participant photos. Use the line **Capture it. Don’t post it.**

### 8. A real village, not a resort

Show that the base belongs to a living rural community: an old house or simple base, garden, tents, craftspeople, food, agriculture, music, and knowledge shared across generations.

Avoid romanticizing poverty, treating local people as scenery, or presenting the Carpathians as an empty wilderness. The project is rooted in and respectful of its community.

### 9. Practical field notes

Present the known facts clearly:

- ages 18–25;
- 10–12 participants and two hosts;
- one week;
- Romanian Carpathians;
- common language: English;
- international participation;
- shared cooking and camp responsibilities;
- one-night mountain expedition;
- essential expedition equipment provided;
- pilot dates and price to be announced.

Include a concise safety note without making unsupported claims. Do not imply medical, legal, or operational guarantees that have not been established.

### 10. FAQ

Answer the questions a participant or parent would actually ask:

- Do I have to give up my phone completely?
- Can my family reach me?
- Do I need hiking or camping experience?
- What equipment is provided?
- How fit do I need to be?
- Is this survival training?
- What language will we speak?
- Who leads the workshops and expedition?
- What are the dates and price?
- How can I hear about the pilot?

When the source material does not provide an answer, be transparent and mark it as “details coming with pilot registration” rather than inventing policy.

### 11. Final invitation

Close with a warm, decisive invitation rather than exaggerated transformation language.

Suggested headline:

**10 PEOPLE. 7 DAYS. 0 ALGORITHMS DECIDING WHAT COMES NEXT.**

Suggested support:

> The first camp will be small on purpose. Join the pilot list to hear when applications open and help shape what this becomes.

Include a minimal email interest form with clear consent language and a useful success state. Do not request unnecessary personal data.

Finish with:

**LESS DIGITAL. MORE ANALOGUE.**

## Interaction and motion

Motion should feel physical and purposeful:

- a signal indicator quietly drops as the visitor enters the story;
- route lines draw as sections enter view;
- field labels stamp or settle into place;
- contact-sheet images shift slightly like loose prints;
- the analogue switch changes state once, meaningfully;
- modest parallax may suggest landscape depth.

Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`. Avoid scroll hijacking, long intro animations, fake loading screens, excessive cursor effects, autoplay audio, or interactions that obscure content.

Create small moments of dry humor in microcopy, such as:

- “Your phone will survive a week without you.”
- “No Wi-Fi. Good company.”
- “You may experience boredom.”
- “Bring boots. Leave your feed behind.”

Use these sparingly so the page retains emotional range.

## UX, accessibility, and technical quality

- Build mobile-first and make the experience excellent from 320px through large desktop screens.
- Use semantic HTML, logical heading order, landmarks, keyboard-accessible controls, visible focus states, useful alt text, and properly labeled form fields.
- Meet WCAG AA contrast requirements.
- Keep body copy comfortably readable and avoid oversized display text that breaks on small screens.
- Optimize images and fonts; reserve media dimensions to prevent layout shift.
- Aim for strong Core Web Vitals and a fast first load on mobile connections.
- Use progressive enhancement. The story and form must remain understandable if animation fails.
- If implementing a real form endpoint is outside scope, provide a polished validated front-end flow and clearly identify the integration point.
- Reuse the project’s existing framework, conventions, and components if present. Do not replace the stack without a compelling reason.

## Copy rules

Write in a voice that is short, clear, intelligent, grounded, occasionally playful, and never preachy.

Prefer concrete verbs and sensory detail: carry, cook, build, walk, listen, argue, laugh, get wet, wake up, pack, help.

Avoid:

- “transform your life”;
- “find your authentic self”;
- “escape the modern world”;
- “once-in-a-lifetime journey”;
- generic wellness language;
- scolding visitors about screen time;
- claims that seven days will solve loneliness, anxiety, or uncertainty.

Do not overfill the page with copy. Preserve breathing room and let imagery, typography, and sequence carry part of the story.

## Definition of done

The finished landing page should:

- feel specific to Unplug to Reconnect and impossible to confuse with a generic retreat template;
- communicate the premise, audience, location, group size, and nature of the pilot within the first two sections;
- make the rhythm of the week tangible without pretending the curriculum is final;
- clearly explain the phone approach, expedition, international character, and shared responsibilities;
- balance youthful adventure with practical trust and safety;
- use the supplied brand palette and the condensed/humanist/monospace type system coherently;
- have one obvious conversion path to join the pilot list;
- avoid fabricated facts and visibly handle unknown dates, pricing, and policies;
- be fully responsive, accessible, performant, and robust with reduced motion;
- include thoughtful empty, loading, validation, success, and error states for the interest form;
- look art-directed at every breakpoint rather than merely rearranged.

Before considering the work complete, review the page against the original concept and visual identity, test every interactive element with keyboard and touch, inspect mobile and desktop layouts, and remove anything that feels like a stock travel website.
